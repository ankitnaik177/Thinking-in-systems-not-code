Late Swinger

I got out to a ball that pitched on middle and hit off stump. What could I have done differently? Let me check with some simulations.

Ankit Naik  ·  Thinking in Systems  ·  August 2026  ·  Download PDF
The 60-second version

If you read nothing else

  • The finding — length barely changes how much an outswinger moves. Full, good or short, it swings the same 0.38 m. What length changes is how much of that movement lands after the bounce — perhaps that is what creates the illusion of late swing.
  • Why it matters — you are not judging a variable amount of movement. You are judging a starting line and adding a near-constant offset.
  • The mechanism — the sideways force is aerodynamic. It is set by pace and seam angle. It does not know where the ball bounced.
  • Do this — meet it further out. Take guard outside the crease, stride to the pitch of the ball, or both — it halves the movement that arrives after you have committed.

So what

Stop trying to predict how much it will swing. Predict the line it started on, add about a ball and a half towards the off side, and meet it as far out as you can so that less of the movement happens after you have committed.

03  Where to meet it

Movement accumulates with distance flown. So the question is not how much it swings — it is how much is still to come once you have committed.

  • It also doubles the late movement — 51 mm becomes 102 mm. A cricket ball is 71 mm in diameter, so that is the difference between the middle of the bat and an edge.

The takeaway

Judge the line.
Meet it early.

I went looking for a way to predict the swing and found there was nothing to predict — it is very nearly the same every ball. What varies is where the bowler aimed, and how much of the movement is still ahead of me when I commit.

  • Read the release and the pitch, then allow about a ball and a half to the off side.
  • Meet it as close to its pitch as you can — guard outside the crease, a full stride, or both. That halves the late movement.
  • Do not play across it. Bat and ball are already diverging.
  • Stop treating "it swung late" as the explanation. It swung all the way.

Method & limits

The numbers here come from one configuration: a new ball at 119 km/h, seam angled 20°, released 2.2 m up. Read the limits before you trust the conclusions.

  • Movement off the seam at the bounce is not modelled. The ball is a smooth sphere, so the pitch gives bounce and friction but cannot catch a raised seam. That effect is length-dependent, and it is the one thing that would undercut Chapter 1.
  • Aerodynamic swing only — drag, a seam-driven side force, and a Magnus force from backspin.
  • Surface state is a single number, set here to a new ball. Reverse swing exists in the model but is switched off for this study.
  • The model only says where the ball is met, not how you get there. Taking guard outside the crease and striding to the pitch reach the same point. It says nothing about what standing out costs you — being stumped, or handing the bowler a different effective length.
  • The side-force coefficients are semi-empirical — chosen to reproduce observed magnitudes, not fitted to actual data.

How it was built

  1. Ball flight and bounce as a Modelica model — multibody dynamics with a contact model for the pitch, solved in Wolfram System Modeler.
  2. Aerodynamics as a custom component: drag, seam side force, Magnus lift, fed by the ball's own velocity and spin.
  3. Trajectories exported and rendered in Blender, driven headlessly from the simulated data.
  4. The dotted line in every figure is the same delivery with the side force set to zero — the honest control.
