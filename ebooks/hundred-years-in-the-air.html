A Hundred Years
in the Air

The Swedish Air Force turns 100 this weekend, and the air show in Linköping is themed past, present and future. This note flies the century in seven simulations.

Overview

The century in seven aircraft

  • The idea. Fly the century in order, one simulation per era: a glider, a piston trainer, a jet airliner, an interceptor, an autopilot, an electric trainer and a drone.
  • The pattern. Each aircraft fixes the biggest problem of the one before it and brings a new problem of its own. The century is that handover, repeated seven times.
  • The numbers. Every figure in this book comes out of the simulations: the same models, flown, measured and plotted.
  • Why now. The show's future segment is drones and satellites: aircraft that get designed and tested in simulation before any metal is cut. This book shows a small part of how that works.

The seven problems

The glider had no engine. The piston engine ran out of air at altitude; the jet fixed that and burned fuel at 2.9 tonnes an hour. The interceptor bought speed that lasted minutes. The autopilot took over the hands, the battery brought back the oldest problem of energy on board, and the drone removed the pilot.

Chapter 1 · 1900s–1920s

01  Altitude is the only fuel

A hundred years ago you flew by borrowing height: Lilienthal ran off hills, the Wrights glided from dunes, the 1920s clubs bungeed gliders off hilltops. I released one in simulation at 1,000 m and let the stick go.

ReleasedGlide speedSinkStays upReaches
In trim, 31 m/s29 m/s steady1.1 m/s14 min 39 s23.1 km
15 m/s too fastswings 12–46 m/s—porpoises every 15 s, height swinging ±90 m
  • The glider is a Schweizer SGS 1-36, a 1980 design that flies by the era's rule: height in, distance out. Trimmed right it flies itself, 25 m forward for every meter of height.
  • Released too fast, it porpoises: climbs, slows, dives, speeds up, climbs again, every 15 seconds. Gliders do this on their own; the pattern is called the phugoid.
  • Either way it ends on the ground once the height is spent. An engine removes that limit.

Chapter 2 · 1930s–1940s

02  The engine breathes

With a 60 kW piston engine on the same class of light airframe, height can be bought back, up to the altitude where the air gets too thin.

  • Full throttle from 500 m, the trainer climbs at 5.9 m/s.
  • A piston engine breathes the same air the wing flies in, so its power falls as the air thins with altitude.
  • By 4,000 m half the climb is gone. At 9,000 m the engine is down to 18 kW, just enough to overcome drag with nothing left to climb on. That altitude is the ceiling.
The era's limit

A bigger piston engine loses power with altitude in the same way. The fix is an engine that carries its own compressor and squeezes the thin air back to a density it can burn: the jet of the next chapter.

Chapter 3 · 1950s–1960s

03  Thin air becomes the point

The jet's compressor keeps feeding the engine as the air thins, and thin air has less drag, so cruising high becomes the efficient choice. I put a DC-8 with four turbojets at 11,000 m and Mach 0.82 and left the controls alone.

  • It cruises above the piston engine's ceiling, covering the glider's whole flight every 95 seconds.
  • It burns three tonnes of fuel an hour. With nobody holding the altitude, it also porpoises for the whole hour, so something has to fly it.

Chapter 4 · 1950s–1970s

04  Climb becomes a weapon

For 1950s Sweden the problem was time: unknown aircraft over the Baltic at 11,000 m, and only minutes to meet them. That job shaped the J 35 Draken. I scrambled a Draken-class delta (49 m² of wing, 78 kN of afterburning turbojet) from 500 m.

7.2 min
From 500 m to the bombers' 11,000 m
12×
Faster to 8 km than the piston trainer of chapter 2
30%
Of its internal fuel burned just getting up there
  • The speed costs twice: thin delta wings hold little fuel, and the afterburner burns 1.3 kg of it every second. Reaching the bombers eats 30% of the tank.
  • The interceptor has minutes of fuel and one very busy pilot doing intercept sums at nine kilometers a minute. Handing some of the flying to a machine helps with both.

Chapter 5 · 1960s–1990s

05  The hands come off

This autopilot is three simple control loops, each reading one sensor and moving one control surface. I gave the chapter-1 glider a set and asked for a 30° left turn.

  • It rolls in, flies the turn and holds the new heading within two degrees for the rest of the flight.
  • With the stick fixed instead, the same aircraft goes wherever its trim points and eventually starts weaving, as the glider did at the end of chapter 1.
  • Feedback loops like these damped the DC-8's porpoising and took workload off the interceptor pilot.
What three PID loops replace is the pilot's patience.

Chapter 6 · 2010s–today

06  The fuel problem returns

Pipistrel sells the same little two-seater twice: the Alpha Trainer burns petrol, the Alpha Electro carries a battery. The two share wings and weight. I flew both at the same speed and compared how fast each used its energy.

Same airframeEnergy on boardEnduranceLands
Alpha Trainer · 36 kg avgas≈1,570 MJ6 h 50 min36 kg lighter
Alpha Electro · 22 kWh battery79 MJ1 h 3 minsame weight it took off at
  • A kilogram of petrol holds about 70 times more energy than a kilogram of this battery. The motor is far more efficient, which recovers some of that, but the petrol plane still flies 6.8 hours to the battery's one.
  • The electric motor needs no air, so its power holds with altitude. At 9,000 m, where chapter 2's climb died, the electric version is still climbing at 3.9 m/s (figure 2). As with the glider, the flight lasts as long as the energy it took off with.

Chapter 7 · 2020s →

07  Remove the pilot

The last aircraft weighs a few kilograms and nobody is aboard. Its battery holds 60 Wh, about a laptop's worth, and an autopilot flies the whole mission.

  • Told to hold 1,000 m, turn 30° and keep 12 m/s, it holds altitude within half a meter and heading within a third of a degree until the battery runs out, about an hour later.
  • It is chapter 5's autopilot plus one more loop, the throttle. With that loop closed, no pilot is needed on board.
  • The air show's future segment is drones and satellites. Once the pilot is software, the aircraft can be any size the mission needs.

the last era's limitation

Gliders borrowed height. The piston engine added power until the air thinned, and the jet's compressor fixed that at a high fuel burn. The interceptor's speed lasted minutes, feedback loops took over holding the course, the battery brought back the glider's fixed energy budget, and the drone left the pilot on the ground. Every aircraft flying at Malmen this weekend descends from one of these steps.

Method & limits

Every number in this study comes from a six-degree-of-freedom flight simulation in Wolfram System Modeler, using one Modelica aircraft library. Read the limits before trusting the conclusions.

  • The aircraft are calibrated replicas. They are not certified models. The glider and both Alphas trace to published data of real aircraft; the DC-8 and the J 35-class delta carry known simplifications (an unswept wing on one, elevons modeled as a small tail surface on the other), so treat their numbers as era-typical, not type-accurate.
  • Hands-fixed flight is eventually unstable in roll, as it is in reality; even the trimmed glider ends its 15-minute flight weaving. Powered scenarios add one proportional bank-to-aileron feedback, a wing leveler standing in for the pilot's lateral corrections; the interceptor also carries a proportional speed-hold pitch loop, the climb schedule a pilot would fly. The glider chapters fly hands-off.
  • Piston vs electric endurance compares the model pair as built: same airframe, same takeoff mass, cruise at the same speed and altitude. Battery mass is inside the airframe's fixed mass; fuel mass burns off. The 70× energy-per-kilogram figure uses the real Alpha Electro pack's published mass of ≈126 kg; the model does not compute it.
  • Each engine is one equation: a density lapse for the piston, a Mach/altitude map for the turbojet and an efficiency chain for the electric motor. That is enough for ceilings and endurance but too coarse for detailed performance charts.
  • Climb and endurance use flight-test method. Climb rate comes from short sawtooth segments at each altitude; endurance from the measured steady cruise burn rate. The DC-8 also needed a large fixed trim deflection, a consequence of its documented undersized elevator.

How it was built

  1. Airframes, engines, sensors and autopilots from the FixedWing Modelica library; scenarios assembled as small top-level models.
  2. Simulated in Wolfram System Modeler (DASSL), compiled and run headlessly through its kernel; plots and analysis from the raw result trajectories.
  3. Atmosphere: US Standard Atmosphere 1976 throughout.
  4. Engine lapse laws: Gagg-Ferrar (piston), σ0.7 (turboprop reference), thrust-ratio map at the design point (turbojet).
